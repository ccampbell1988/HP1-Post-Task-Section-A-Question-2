Part A: GMP Webinar Reviews

Webinar 1 title

Athlete Identity: Creative and Dynamic (Gold Medal Pathway, National Team Technical Curriculum)

This one really got me thinking about how I coach skill. The big message was that being "creative and dynamic" isn't just for the flashy, highly skilled kids. It's about reading the play and using deception to buy yourself and your teammates more time and space. That's something every player on my team can get better at, including my grinders.

Key Takeaway 1: Creativity starts with the boring stuff: skating and edges

What I learned

The webinar was really clear that creative and dynamic skills come from a strong foundation: small-space skating, edge work, balance and puck control. Outside edge engagement was called out as a key factor, and the message was "don't rush through your ABCs." Honestly, I'm guilty of moving on too fast once players look comfortable.

How I will implement it

Every practice will start with a 10-minute small-space block: tight turns, cutbacks, escapes, outside-edge work and stop-and-gos, first without pucks and then with them. I want players balanced and mobile at slower speeds before we shrink the time and space.

When / evidence

From the first week of the season, in every practice. I'll know it's working when our Skating and Plays with Speed scores go up at the midseason SkillShark evaluation.

Key Takeaway 2: "Every drill is a good drill" if it checks four boxes

What I learned

I loved this one. A drill doesn't need to be fancy. It just needs a technical element, a tactical element, a read-and-react element and a compete element, and it has to transfer directly to the game.

How I will implement it

I'm going back through my practice plans and running every drill through those four filters. If a drill is just a pattern, I'll add a read: a defender who makes a choice, a coach who calls where the puck goes, or a second puck to compete for. My feedback will also focus more on the small details, like whether they actually used an escape turn or a fake, and not just whether the puck went in.

When / evidence

During practice planning all season. Every drill on my practice plans will show which of the four elements it hits.

Key Takeaway 3: Teach the when, where and why, not just the how

What I learned

The skills inventory (small-space skating, cutbacks and escapes, no-looks and look-offs, stick and feet fakes, stop-and-gos, micro moves) applies in all three zones, not just the offensive zone. The point is to manipulate defenders by making them think you're going one way when you're really going the other.

How I will implement it

We'll use short video clips to show where these moves happen in real games: a cutback to escape a forechecker on a breakout, a look-off on the power play, a fake on a D-to-D. I'll also try a "move of the week" that we teach in practice and then call out when someone uses it in a game. Players need to know it's okay to try things and fail, so I'll keep the environment safe for creativity.

When / evidence

Weekly video and move-of-the-week from October on. I'll know it's working when players start using the moves in games without being told.

Overall reflection: how this webinar changes my coaching

We're a hard-working, gritty team, and this webinar reminded me that grit and creativity aren't opposites. A gritty player who can escape pressure on the wall and make the next play is exactly what we want.

Webinar 2 title

Athlete Identity: Good Sticks (Gold Medal Pathway, National Team Technical Curriculum)

This one hit home for me because sticks are something every single player can be good at, no matter their size or skill level. The message was simple: a good stick helps you on offence, on defence and around the net. It doesn't cost anything, and it's all habit.

Key Takeaway 1: A "good stick" means three different jobs

What I learned

I always just said "stick on the ice," but this broke it down way better. A good stick means being a passing option with a target your teammates can hit, having stick on puck to create loose pucks for transition, and being net-front ready for tips, deflections and rebounds. Those are three different jobs and we need all of them.

How I will implement it

"Show me a target" is becoming a standard call in practice: no pass goes to a player who isn't presenting their blade. Every practice will also have a net-front station where players work tips, deflections and keeping their stick free for rebounds.

When / evidence

Starting in the early season and kept up all year. I'll know it's working when fewer passes get bobbled in practice and we get more second-chance goals in games.

Key Takeaway 2: Stick on puck first, separation second, and it's built in practice

What I learned

The webinar was clear that good stick habits are built in practice, starting stationary before moving into game-like reps. It also said to split players into smaller groups by position to get specific. The order matters too: stick on puck first, then separate the player from the puck.

How I will implement it

I'll run short stationary stick stations (lifts, pokes, sweeps, presses) before we move into 1-on-1 angling. Once a week I'll split by position: the D work stick in lanes and steering, and the forwards work backchecking lifts and tips. This fits right into the angling drills in our Defensive Team Play plan.

When / evidence

Weekly positional stick blocks from October on. Our SkillShark Defensive Team Play and Hockey IQ scores both include stick use, so the midseason evaluation will show whether it's working.

Key Takeaway 3: Make yourself big: sticks take away lanes

What I learned

I loved the reminder that a good stick isn't just about taking the puck. It's about making yourself look big, taking away passing lanes and steering opponents to the outside. More stick-on-puck means more loose pucks, and that means more transition and more possession for us.

How I will implement it

We'll use short video clips of sticks taking away lanes and steering players to the outside, especially on the penalty kill and in the neutral zone. I'll also start tracking "stick takeaways" on our game card so players see that good sticks get rewarded and not just goals.

When / evidence

Game-card tracking from November on. I'll know it's working when our stick takeaways go up and opponents get fewer clean passes through the middle.

Overall reflection: how this webinar changes my coaching

Our team's biggest gap right now is transition, and this webinar made me realize good sticks are where a lot of transition starts. If we're creating more loose pucks, we're getting more chances to go the other way. Plus it's a habit we can coach every single day.

Part B: Building a Positive Team Culture

Culture is what players experience every day, not what is written on the dressing room wall. My plan is built on the values co-created with players (Respect, Commitment, Accountability, Team First, and Growth & Resilience) and is reinforced through daily behaviours, consistent staff modelling, and regular reflection.

Our Culture Promise

Every player feels known, valued and challenged. Standards are clear, shared and owned by the players. Staff model the same preparation, respect and emotional control we expect from the group.

Prepared. Honest. Supportive. Courageous.

How the Culture Is Built

  • Co-created charter: players turn the values into observable behaviours at the September values workshop.
  • Connection first: rotating partners, small-group check-ins and player profile cards prevent cliques.
  • Specific recognition: weekly recognition of effort, improvement and team-first actions, not only results.
  • Healthy conflict: concerns are addressed directly, respectfully and early, using an agreed process.
  • Measured, not assumed: monthly culture-pulse check-ins, a midseason reset and a postseason review.

Belonging

Every player is known. One-on-one meetings in September, and a staff member checks in with each player at least monthly.

Standards

Clear and shared. The team charter and operating agreement are posted, referenced and revisited at the midseason reset.

Safety

Physically and psychologically safe. Zero tolerance for hazing, exclusion or demeaning language; Rule of Two and Safe Sport reporting are followed.

Growth

Mistakes are information. Feedback is specific, private when corrective, and tied to each player's PDP.

Part B: Developing a Leadership Team

Leadership is a skill that is taught and practised, not a title. I will build a formal leadership group and also give every player a leadership responsibility during the season.

1. Selection (September Wk 3)

  • Players complete a peer leadership survey: who do you trust, who holds the standard, who lifts the group?
  • Staff combine survey results with observed behaviours from camp.
  • Leadership group of 4-5 players: captain, alternates and one younger/emerging leader.

2. Roles & Responsibilities

  • Voice of the room between players and staff.
  • Model and protect the charter standards.
  • Lead warm-ups, recovery reminders and pre-game routines.
  • Welcome and integrate new or affiliate players.

3. Leadership Development

  • Bi-weekly 15-minute leadership meeting with the head coach.
  • Monthly topic: communication, giving feedback, handling conflict, leading under pressure, leading by example.
  • Leaders debrief one real situation each month.

4. Shared Leadership

  • Rotating player pairs lead warm-ups and the value-of-the-week message.
  • Community project roles give every player ownership.
  • At least one player-led action every month.

Evaluating Our Culture and Leadership

Monthly culture-pulse surveys (belonging, trust, communication), leadership self and peer feedback at midseason, attendance and punctuality trends, and staff observations of charter behaviours. Results are shared with the leadership group and used to adjust the plan at the midseason reset.
